▾1. Management Overview

This section is written for readers who need to understand the process and its value without operating the system. It can be read on its own.

1.1 What bank reconciliation is, and why it matters

Bank reconciliation is the process of proving that the cash balance recorded in the company's books agrees with the balance reported by the bank and explaining every difference between the two. It is the primary control over cash. It is how an organisation detects a payment that never reached its beneficiary, a receipt that was never recorded, a bank charge nobody authorised, a duplicated payment, or a fraudulent withdrawal.

Because it is a control, it only works if it is done consistently and completely. A reconciliation performed once a month, in a hurry, on a spreadsheet, will usually balance — but it will not reliably tell you why it balances, and it will not catch a problem quickly enough to do anything about it.

1.2 Where the effort goes today

Under a manual process, effort is spread across every single transaction rather than concentrated on the ones that need attention:

  • The statement is obtained as a PDF or printout, and its lines are keyed or pasted into a spreadsheet.
  • The corresponding D365 transaction list is exported and pasted alongside.
  • Both sides are sorted and compared by eye, line by line, and ticked off.
  • Differences are highlighted by hand and chased individually.
  • Bank-initiated items such as charges and interest are posted separately through a general journal.
  • The summary is rebuilt each period, and the spreadsheet becomes the audit record.

The cost is not only time. Transcription errors, stale exports, inconsistent treatment between preparers, and a reconciliation that exists only in one person's workbook are all consequences of the manual approach. Because it is slow, it also tends to be done once, at month-end — which means a problem in the first week of the month is discovered five weeks later.

1.3 What advanced bank reconciliation changes

Figure 1 · Manual reconciliation compared with advanced bank reconciliation in D365 F&O

The change is not that the system does the reconciliation for you. It is that the system does the matching for you, so that people spend their time only on the items that genuinely need judgement.

FOR MANAGEMENT · The single most important point

Under the new process the user no longer looks at every line. The system pairs off everything that matches the agreed rules and presents only the exceptions. The reconciliation becomes an exception review rather than a full comparison.

1.4 End-to-end process flow

Figure 2 · Advanced bank reconciliation — end-to-end process flow

Reading the diagram from the top: the bank produces an MT940 statement file and delivers it. D365 imports the file, checks that it is internally consistent and belongs to the right bank account, and creates a reconciliation worksheet. The matching rules run automatically. Everything they match is done. Everything they cannot match goes to a finance user, who either matches it manually, posts it, or consciously leaves it open as a timing difference. The user then marks the reconciliation as reconciled, which updates the general ledger and locks the statement.

NOTE · On quantifying the saving

The realistic saving depends on how well the matching rules are tuned and on how clean the underlying data is. A well-configured account where most payments carry a usable reference will typically auto-match the large majority of lines on the first pass. Accounts dominated by cheques and cash deposits, where the bank narrative carries no reference D365 can key on, will auto-match a smaller proportion. The rules should be reviewed after the first two or three live periods and tightened using what the exceptions actually show.

If Modern is to be enabled on any additional legal entity or environment, do it as part of the initial setup rather than mid-stream.

2.3 Supported statement formats

D365 supports three electronic bank statement formats out of the box — ISO 20022 CAMT.053, MT940 and BAI2 — and the framework can be extended to others. This engagement uses MT940, the SWIFT end-of-day customer statement message, because that is what the bank supplies.

Figure 4 · Quick identification checklist — telling the common statement formats apart

If you are ever handed a statement file and are not certain what it is, open it in a text editor. An MT940 file is plain text, begins with a {1: header block and contains colon-delimited tags such as :20:, :25: and :61:. A CAMT.053 file is XML. A BAI2 file is plain text but comma-delimited and begins with numeric record codes.

2.4 Understanding the MT940 file

A finance user does not need to read MT940 fluently but understanding the handful of tags that drive the import makes troubleshooting far quicker — particularly when a statement fails to import or the balances do not validate.

2.4.1 The tags that matter most

Tag

The critical tag. The value before the separator must equal the Routing number on the D365 bank account; the value after it must equal the Bank account number. This is how the import decides which account the statement belongs to.

2.4.2 Account identification — the most common cause of a failed import

The import does not ask you which account a statement belongs to; it works it out from the file. If the values in :25: do not exactly match the bank account master, the statement will not import, or will import against the wrong account.

Figure 6 · The :25: tag split into routing number and bank account number

CAUTION · Check this first when an import fails

Open the file, find the :25: line, and compare both halves against Cash and bank management > Bank accounts > the account > Routing number and Bank account number. Leading zeros matter. A currency suffix in the file (for example INR at the end) is not part of the account number.

2.4.3 multi-block statement files

A single MT940 file frequently contains more than one statement block. Each block runs from :20: to a closing balance tag. Where a block closes with :62M: and the next opens with :60M:, the two blocks are continuations of the same statement — the balance is carried forward rather than closed.

The statement supplied with this engagement is exactly this shape: block one closes :62M:D210730INR494364,00 and block two opens :60M:D210730INR494364,00 before closing finally with :62F:.

:28C:00165/00001 <- block 1, sequence 1

:60F:C210729INR0,00 <- true opening balance

... statement lines ...

:62M:D210730INR494364,00 <- intermediate closing balance

:28C:00165/00002 <- block 2, sequence 2

:60M:D210730INR494364,00 <- intermediate opening, carried forward

... statement lines ...

:62F:D210730INR1010614,51 <- final closing balance

IMPORTANT · Never split a multi-block file

Importing the blocks separately will break the opening/closing balance validation, because block two's opening balance will not equal the closing balance of the last fully closed statement. Always import the file exactly as the bank delivered it.

By default, a matching rule matches to the first D365 transaction that meets its criteria. Where several payments share the same amount and date — which is common with round-sum vendor payments — the rule will pick one arbitrarily and it may be the wrong one. Forcing these to manual review costs a few minutes and prevents a class of error that is very difficult to unwind.

Lines that carry forward will keep carrying forward until someone deals with them. Review the age of unmatched lines every week. An item still unmatched after a month is not a timing difference — it is an unresolved problem.

6.1 Debit and credit orientation is inverted

6.1.1 The symptom

A transaction the bank reports as a debit appears in D365 as a debit, when it should appear as a credit — and vice versa. The result is that statement lines and D365 bank transactions sit on opposite sides and will not match.

The cause is a difference in perspective. The bank reports from its own point of view: money leaving your account is a debit to the bank's liability to you. Your ledger records the same movement as a credit to the bank account. MT940 carries the bank's orientation.

Figure 61 · Microsoft LCS issue 3247038 — the behaviour is by design

Microsoft closed this as working as designed, with the observation that the transformation should be customised where a different orientation is required.

Figure 63 · After correction — the orientations align and matching succeeds

GOOD PRACTICE · Use the parameter, not a code change

Neither is necessary. The Reverse debit credit mark parameter on the bank account Reconciliation FastTab performs exactly this inversion natively.

6.4 Transactions posted in D365 before they reach the bank

A reconciliation can only match what exists on both sides. Where a transaction has been posted in D365 but has not yet reached the bank, it will appear as an unmatched D365 bank transaction until the statement carrying it arrives.

This is normal and is the definition of a timing difference — but it was raised during UAT as a concern, so the treatment should be explicit:

Scenario

Treatment

Cheque issued, not yet presented

Leave unmatched. It carries forward and matches when the bank clears it. This is a genuine reconciling item and should appear as such on the reconciliation statement.

Post-dated cheques

Where the journal entry is posted only once the bank has confirmed receipt of funds, no reconciling item arises. Where entries are posted on receipt of the instrument, they will sit unmatched until clearance.

Payment posted in D365 that the bank has rejected

The payment must be reversed in D365. It will never appear on a statement.

Entry held or blocked at the bank for any reason

Leave unmatched and track it. If the bank ultimately does not process it, reverse the D365 entry.

KNOWN GAP · Open item carried forward from UAT

A list of scenarios where entries are posted in D365 before bank confirmation — excluding post-dated cheques — was requested during UAT and has not yet been supplied. Until it is, the volume of permanently unmatched D365-side transactions cannot be estimated, and matching rules cannot be tuned for them. See section 7.3.

6.5 Subledger as offset on a new transaction

KNOWN GAP · New transactions cannot offset to a subledger

The "post new transactions" route does not support a subledger — customer or vendor — as the offset account. Under Modern bank reconciliation this is no longer a real constraint: use Generate payment journal for anything involving a customer or vendor, and reserve Generate voucher for genuine general ledger postings such as fees and interest.

6.6 Enabling advanced reconciliation on an account with history

D365 will not enable advanced bank reconciliation on an account that still carries unreconciled transactions from the previous process. This is the principal cutover constraint and is addressed in section 7.

▾7. Cutover and Go-Live Readiness

7.1 The cutover constraint

Advanced bank reconciliation cannot be enabled on a bank account that still carries unreconciled transactions from the manual process. Every existing transaction on the account must be cleared first. Two approaches are available.

Option A — clear to a cut-off date

Option B — new bank account master

Approach

Agree a cut-off date. Reconcile or clear all transactions up to it using the existing process, then enable advanced reconciliation from the day after.

Create a new bank account master, enable advanced reconciliation on it, and direct new transactions there. The old account is run down and closed.

Advantages

One continuous bank account. History and reporting stay in one place. No change to bank details in masters or payment files.

No dependency on clearing the backlog. Go-live date is not held hostage to historic items.

Disadvantages

Requires the backlog to be cleared, which may be substantial and may itself hold up go-live.

Two accounts to manage during transition. Vendor and customer bank details, payment formats and integrations may reference the old account.

Best when

The backlog is small and can realistically be cleared.

The backlog is large, old, or cannot be cleared without write-offs.

IMPORTANT · This decision drives the go-live date

Take it early. Under Option A the go-live date depends on how quickly the backlog can be cleared, which is a finance workload question rather than a system one. Under Option B the go-live date is independent of the backlog, but there is integration and master data work to schedule. The decision should be closed before the build is signed off.
